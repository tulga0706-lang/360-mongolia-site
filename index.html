Our camps

Our 360° trip style

On all our journeys you will stay at our private 360° ger camps.

Our camps are mobile. They are collapsible five-star pop-up camps that deploy anywhere in Mongolia. We can load your private, stylish ger camp onto a truck or onto a caravan of yak or camel carts. The camp sites are carefully chosen, with 360° landscape views, next to a nomadic family or anywhere in the great Mongolian void. You may move on foot, on camel or on horseback through the landscapes with the camp folded. Minimum stay is 2 overnights for each place, for a maximum of 6 guests. You will sleep in a traditional Mongolian ger, simply but stylishly decorated with colourful Mongolian crafts and furnished with proper beds and full bedding. Enjoy a private 360 degrees landscape view!

You will dine in a separate dining ger or traditional Mongolian marquee-style tent. Compost-style toilets and a safari-style shower are also set up in traditional Mongolian tents.

Our camp entourage always includes a skilled cook and a camp master. On relocation days the entire camp can be dismantled, packed onto our support vehicle, or loaded onto a caravan of yak or camel carts, and set up again in the new location.

Occasionally – if at all available – we will also make use of the best tourist ger camps and lodges in the region.

About us

360° Mongolia is a collection of extraordinary, bespoke experiences curated by Nomadic Journeys, a pioneer in Mongolian sustainable travel since 1993.

Our Travel Philosophy

Our approach to travel focuses on regions where we have built long-term partnerships with local nomadic communities and active conservation initiatives. These handpicked destinations feature what can only be described as 360° landscapes—unspoiled, horizon-to-horizon vistas that offer complete immersion in another world.

To explore these remote wildernesses in comfort and style, we utilize small, mobile private camps made up of beautifully appointed, traditional Mongolian gers (yurts). This low-impact setup allows us to set up comfortable camps anywhere, oftentimes in partnership with locals. You will tread lightly while delivering an authentic, high-end expedition experience.

Where possible, we partner with an emerging number of vetted new on-grid properties across the country.

Heritage & Vision

Nomadic Journeys was founded in Mongolia in 1993, with a shared vision to open an entirely new frontier for global ecotourism.

Since 2017, 360° Mongolia has operated independently under Natural AB, a Swedish entity established by Jan Wigsten in 1991. For decades, Wigsten has served as an international sustainable tourism consultant for global organizations—including Conservation International, WWF, The World Bank, and CBI Netherlands—connecting indigenous communities to sustainable tourism livelihoods across East Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean, Scandinavia, and the Indian subcontinent.

Hunting with eagles

A Kazakh horseman rides across a snow-covered valley in Mongolia’s Altai Mountains, a golden eagle balanced on his forearm. The bird’s weight is carried by a wooden rest fixed to the saddle. A leather hood, called a tomaga, covers its eyes. When the rider spots a fox, he lifts the hood and the eagle takes flight.

Kazakh families in western Mongolia have passed down the skills of hunting with eagles for generations. Rock carvings in the Altai have been interpreted as early depictions of the practice, dating back 3000 years. Chinese chronicles also describe hunting with birds of prey.

Some hunters collect a young eagle from a cliff nest in summer. They generally prefer females, which are larger than males. Over the following months, a hunter feeds the eagle by hand, teaches it to respond to his voice, and gradually familiarizes the eagle to the movements of a horse. By winter, when foxes have grown their thick coats, the pair can cover long stretches of open country in search of game. Red foxes and corsacs are prized for their fur, which is used for winter clothing. Hunters also take hares, and there are accounts of several hunters flying their eagles together at a wolf.

After several seasons, a hunter will return the eagle to the wild, where it can reproduce while still strong. The release marks the end of years spent learning the habits of an individual bird. It is one of the quieter moments in a tradition best known for its drama. UNESCO first inscribed falconry, a practice shared by Mongolia and other countries, on its List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010.

Today, there are multiple eagle festivals in different Kazakh communities across Bayan Ölgii province, ongoing for a month and starting as early as the 2nd or 3rd weekend in September, as well as adjacent to the Nauryz spring equinox festival in March. These bring hunters together from across the region and give visitors a chance to see how horse, rider, and eagle work together.
